# Graph Neural Networks for Semi-Supervised Node Classification: Foundations, Architectures, Scalability, and Evaluation

Kalhar Patel · Dev Mulchandani · Deep Dhaduk · Nikita

## Abstract

Graph neural networks (GNNs) learn representations from node attributes and graph connectivity by repeatedly exchanging information along edges. Their success in semi-supervised node classification stems from the ability to exploit relational structure even when relatively few nodes carry observed labels. Yet the term GNN encompasses models with different assumptions about neighborhood homogeneity, expressive capacity, computational complexity, the availability of test nodes at training time, and the meaning of benchmark accuracy. This tutorial surveys ten peer-reviewed papers that established important directions in this field: graph convolutional networks (GCNs), inductive neighborhood sampling, attention-based aggregation, expressivity analysis, simplified convolution, personalized PageRank propagation, over-smoothing theory, graph-subgraph sampling, large-scale evaluation, and heterogeneous neighborhood geometry. The survey reconstructs the shared message-passing viewpoint, explains the mathematical and experimental differences among the methods, and assesses the conditions under which their reported advantages are informative. A reproducible two-layer GCN implemented in PyTorch Geometric accompanies the survey as an instructional baseline for Cora, CiteSeer, and PubMed. Rather than presenting unmeasured improvements, the implementation separates model selection from final test evaluation and records metrics for independent replication. The synthesis shows that architectural sophistication alone does not determine performance: graph assortativity, split construction, feature quality, sampling behavior, and system constraints jointly decide whether more expressive or deeper propagation is useful.

Index Terms—graph neural networks, node classification, graph convolution, message passing, semi-supervised learning, representation learning, reproducibility.

## I. Introduction

Many classification problems are relational rather than independent. A scientific publication cites other publications, a user belongs to an interaction network, a protein participates in a biological interaction graph, and a transaction connects accounts, merchants, and devices. In such settings the object to be classified is embedded in a structure that may carry predictive information beyond its measured attributes. Classical supervised methods typically map an attribute vector to a class label without explicitly using edges. Graph learning begins with the proposition that connections themselves encode useful evidence, while recognizing that links may also be noisy, incomplete, misleading, or shaped by the mechanism that created the dataset.

Semi-supervised node classification presents a particularly instructive problem. Each vertex has an attribute vector and a label may be observed for only part of the graph. The objective is to predict labels for the remaining vertices, usually by training a function that combines node attributes with information arriving from adjacent or more distant vertices. This seemingly simple task reveals several difficult design choices. A model must decide which neighbors to use, how their messages should be weighted, whether the same parameters can apply to previously unseen vertices, how far information should travel, and how performance should be estimated when graph edges connect training and evaluation vertices.

The contemporary literature has explored these decisions through a sequence of architectural and methodological changes. Kipf and Welling's graph convolutional network provided a compact, effective approach to smooth features along graph edges [1]. Hamilton, Ying, and Leskovec reframed embedding computation as a learned aggregation function that can transfer to unseen nodes and graphs [2]. Veličković and colleagues introduced attention to make neighborhood contributions data-dependent [3]. Xu and colleagues asked whether existing aggregation functions could distinguish different neighborhood multisets [4]. Wu and colleagues then challenged the assumption that graph representation learning always requires repeated nonlinear transformations [5]. In parallel, Gasteiger (formerly Klicpera) and colleagues separated predictions from longer-range diffusion using personalized PageRank [6].

Later work clarified why depth and scale cannot be treated as routine engineering details. Oono and Suzuki analyzed conditions under which deep graph networks lose node-discriminating information [7]. Zeng and colleagues developed GraphSAINT to train on sampled subgraphs instead of expanding per-layer neighborhoods [8]. Hu and colleagues introduced the Open Graph Benchmark to improve the realism and consistency of graph machine-learning evaluation [9]. Pei and colleagues examined graphs where connected nodes do not usually share labels, challenging the homophily assumptions that favor basic graph convolution [10]. These contributions do not form a simple progression toward one universally superior architecture. They define competing responses to distinct limitations.

This survey organizes that literature around the lifecycle of a node-classification system: problem formulation, information aggregation, inductive generalization, representational limits, propagation depth, scalable computation, and credible evaluation. The papers are discussed in an integrated argument rather than as isolated abstracts. Particular attention is paid to the difference between a result claimed by an original paper and a conclusion that can legitimately be drawn across studies. No new experiment is claimed in this report. The accompanying code is an independently runnable GCN baseline and should be used to produce local empirical results before any comparative claim is made.

## II. Problem Definition and Conceptual Background

Let G=(V,E) denote a graph with n vertices and m edges. The adjacency matrix A records which vertices are connected, and X in R^(n×d) contains d attributes per vertex. For node classification, y_v belongs to a finite set of classes, but labels are available only on a training subset V_train. A neural model f_theta(A,X) outputs class scores or probabilities for every vertex. Its parameters theta are fitted by minimizing a supervised loss computed on observed training labels. Even if the optimizer only uses V_train, many transductive GNNs perform feature propagation over the full graph, including vertices reserved for validation and testing. That distinction is fundamental: access to unlabeled test-node attributes or edges is not equivalent to access to their labels.

In the transductive protocol, the topology and features of the evaluated graph are generally visible during training. The learning task is to infer labels of other vertices within that known structure. The standard citation-network experiments used to motivate GCNs belong to this family. An inductive protocol instead asks a trained model to process new nodes or entire graphs without assigning a separate learned embedding to each training vertex. Induction depends on transferable aggregation functions and compatible attributes; the existence of shared parameters alone does not guarantee strong performance after distribution shift. GraphSAGE made this operational distinction central rather than incidental [2].

A generic message-passing layer updates the representation h_v of node v using a permutation-invariant aggregation of messages from neighbors N(v). One can write h_v^(l+1)=UPDATE_l(h_v^l, AGGREGATE_l({MESSAGE_l(h_v^l,h_u^l,e_uv):u in N(v)})). The notation is deliberately broad. Some models use a normalized sum of neighboring features, some learn attention weights, and others pool transformed messages. Permutation invariance is necessary when an ordering of neighbors is arbitrary, but the chosen invariant operation also limits which distinct neighborhoods can be distinguished. Therefore an aggregation rule is not merely a computational convenience; it expresses an assumption about the information needed for the task [4].

Graph homophily refers to the tendency for linked nodes to have related labels or attributes. If a citation graph connects publications in the same subject, averaging nearby features can reinforce class evidence. In a heterophilous graph, however, neighbors may systematically carry different labels. A simple low-pass smoothing operator can then erase rather than reveal a useful difference. Real graphs combine these regimes, and edge formation may be influenced by latent variables, label imbalance, popularity, or time. It is consequently unsafe to equate graph connectivity with label similarity without measuring the relevant patterns. Geom-GCN explicitly investigates this limitation [10].

A common spectral perspective begins with a graph Laplacian, a matrix that describes variation across edges. Low-frequency graph signals tend to vary slowly between connected vertices, while high-frequency signals change more rapidly. Neighbor averaging favors the former. This offers a useful interpretation of the GCN operator, but frequency language should not be stretched into a complete explanation of every attention or nonlinear model. Learned transformations, finite depth, normalization, and graph topology can substantially alter behavior. The spectral and spatial interpretations are complementary lenses, not a guarantee that a particular model will succeed on a particular graph [1], [5].

Most empirical studies represent categorical labels with cross-entropy and use train, validation, and test masks. The training mask controls parameter updates, the validation mask supports model selection, and the test mask provides a final estimate once decisions are fixed. If hyperparameters are chosen after inspecting test accuracy, the test set is no longer an independent assessment. Repeated trials also introduce variability from initialization, stochastic sampling, and hardware. A responsible comparison therefore reports split definitions, random seeds, model-selection rules, and dispersion across runs instead of presenting a single favorable number as a universal property [9].

## III. The Graph Convolutional Baseline

The graph convolutional network of Kipf and Welling [1] is an appropriate starting point because it makes the interaction between local topology and learned feature transformations explicit. The derivation starts from spectral graph convolution and adopts a localized first-order approximation. Its commonly used propagation rule is H^(l+1)=sigma(D_tilde^(-1/2) A_tilde D_tilde^(-1/2) H^l W^l), where A_tilde=A+I includes self-loops, D_tilde is the corresponding diagonal degree matrix, W^l is trainable, and sigma is a nonlinearity. The normalization reduces the degree-dependent scale of aggregation; including self-loops retains a node's own features when its next representation is computed.

For a two-layer classifier, the first normalized propagation mixes neighboring input features and projects them into a hidden space. A nonlinear activation then creates a learned intermediate representation. A second propagation maps hidden states to class logits. With the standard semi-supervised loss, a small labeled subset can provide gradients to shared parameters that are reused over the entire graph. Two layers give each node access to at most a two-hop receptive field under ordinary local propagation, although the representation and effect of a path depend on normalization, nonlinearities, and intervening nodes. The model is simple enough to debug, which is a major reason to retain it as a reference when evaluating new GNN methods.

The original paper evaluates citation-network classification and motivates its architecture through efficiency and accuracy [1]. Its contribution is not just the numerical outcome on a particular split. It supplies a compact layer that can be implemented as sparse matrix multiplication plus a dense feature transformation. Storage is related to the sparsity of the graph rather than a fully dense n-by-n adjacency matrix. For small citation datasets, full-batch learning is practical and conceptually clean. On very large graphs, however, even a linear dependence on edges can be demanding because intermediate activations, full-graph traversal, and gradient computation compete for memory and bandwidth. This pressure motivated alternative sampling schemes [2], [8].

The GCN layer has an inductive bias: neighboring information is combined using graph-derived weights, not weights selected separately for each relationship. This is beneficial when local smoothing matches the label structure and a drawback when neighbors differ in reliability. Nodes of markedly different degrees also experience different mixing patterns, even after normalization. A high-degree node may aggregate evidence from many heterogeneous contexts, while a low-degree node receives relatively few relational signals. Self-loops mitigate total loss of the focal node's features, but they do not remove the fundamental assumption that local connectivity should influence prediction.

A baseline implementation must distinguish algorithmic fidelity from paper-level reproduction. Re-implementing a two-layer GCN with the same broad hyperparameters is a demonstration of the method, not a guaranteed replication of every historical result. Dataset versions, preprocessing, split construction, early-stopping logic, library kernels, and random initialization can vary. The code accompanying this survey uses public Planetoid masks, feature normalization, 16 hidden channels by default, ReLU, dropout, Adam, and weight decay. It retains the best model according to validation accuracy and evaluates the test mask only after selection. These decisions are documented so that observed outcomes can be interpreted rather than mistaken for unreported experimental evidence.

## IV. From Transductive Convolution to Inductive Aggregation

GraphSAGE, introduced by Hamilton, Ying, and Leskovec [2], changes the object being learned. Earlier graph embedding procedures often associated parameters or representations with a fixed collection of nodes. Such an approach becomes awkward when a platform continuously adds accounts, documents, or products. GraphSAGE instead learns aggregation functions that construct a representation from node features and sampled neighborhoods. A newly appearing vertex can therefore be embedded without fitting an individual parameter vector for that vertex, provided that the necessary features and neighborhood information are available.

The framework proceeds layer by layer. For each node, a bounded sample of neighbors is selected; their current representations are aggregated; and the result is combined with the node's existing representation and transformed. The original work examines mean, pooling, and recurrent aggregation variants. Their differences matter because the same neighbor multiset can be mapped to different statistics. Mean aggregation captures an average neighborhood profile, pooling can emphasize strong responses to learned feature detectors, and a sequence model can encode order-sensitive computations if an ordering is supplied. Since graph neighbors have no natural universal order, order handling requires caution when interpreting recurrent aggregation.

Sampling is central to GraphSAGE's scalability claim. If every layer expands over all neighbors, computation may grow quickly with node degree and depth. Restricting the number of neighbors produces more predictable minibatch computation, although at the cost of sampling noise and a possibly incomplete view of structural signals. Fixed fanouts also change the effective information available to a high-degree node relative to a low-degree node. The right sampling budget is thus both a systems parameter and a modeling choice. More neighbors may improve coverage but increase memory and runtime, while fewer can hurt rare-class or long-range evidence.

The paper evaluates inductive node classification, including cases where new nodes or new graphs arise [2]. This is a stronger and different objective than predicting held-out labels on a known citation graph. One should not compare raw results from these protocols as though they measured the same generalization problem. A model that performs well transductively can rely on graph relationships that will not exist at inference time; an inductive model must construct outputs under the information actually available for new observations. In practical systems, the timing of feature collection and graph updates can be as important as the model architecture itself.

GraphSAGE complements rather than replaces GCNs. A mean aggregator with an appropriate transform resembles local smoothing, while neighbor sampling and learned aggregation enable more flexible deployment. The major lesson is that model identity cannot be separated from the data-access contract. Before selecting GraphSAGE or a full-batch GCN, an engineer should specify whether inference targets are known during training, whether their neighborhoods are accessible, how often the graph changes, and what latency is acceptable. Those requirements determine the appropriate training and evaluation protocols as much as any published accuracy table.

## V. Adaptive Neighbor Weighting with Attention

The graph attention network of Veličković and colleagues [3] addresses the rigidity of assigning aggregation weights solely from graph degree. In GAT, neighboring node representations are linearly transformed, a learned compatibility function computes pairwise scores, and a softmax normalizes those scores over the relevant neighborhood. The resulting representation is a weighted combination of neighboring transformed features. Since attention scores depend on the features and learned parameters, two adjacent nodes may contribute unequally to a target representation even when their graph-theoretic degree normalizations are similar.

A typical attention coefficient can be written alpha_vu=softmax_u(LeakyReLU(a^T[W h_v || W h_u])), where concatenation is denoted by || and a and W are learned parameters. Multi-head attention applies several coefficient mechanisms in parallel, frequently concatenating their outputs in hidden layers and averaging or otherwise combining them at the output. These heads provide multiple learned views of the neighborhood; their presence alone does not establish that every head discovers a distinct, semantically meaningful relation. Attention weights may be useful for diagnostics, but they should not automatically be interpreted as a faithful causal explanation of predictions.

The main appeal of GAT is flexibility without requiring expensive eigendecompositions or an explicit spectral basis [3]. Attention offers a route to modeling neighbors that differ in relevance, which can be valuable when edge existence is not a sufficient indicator of information quality. Yet the attention mechanism does not solve noisy topology by itself. The model still aggregates over a neighborhood defined by supplied edges, and relevant nodes beyond that neighborhood remain inaccessible unless extra layers or graph transformations bring them into range. The usefulness of attention also depends on informative features: if several neighbors are indistinguishable in the features available to the scoring function, their relative weights may be difficult to learn.

Relative to a normalized GCN, attention adds computation for edge-level scores and their normalization. This may be modest for sparse, small graphs but consequential on large or highly connected networks. Multi-head structures increase the amount of intermediate data held or processed, and an implementation's memory characteristics depend on sparse kernels and edge representation. Evaluating GAT therefore requires recording hardware and measurement methodology, not simply reporting parameter counts. A model with fewer dense weights can still incur higher edge-processing cost.

GAT illustrates a broader tradeoff. Replacing fixed topology-based weights with adaptive coefficients expands modeling flexibility, but that flexibility introduces more degrees of freedom and additional opportunities for overfitting on small labeled sets. Where a simple GCN works reliably, improvements should be tested against a tuned baseline rather than attributed to attention as a generic advantage. The contribution of the attention paper is the proposal and evaluation of a learnable neighborhood weighting mechanism; it does not imply that attention dominates simple convolution across arbitrary graph regimes [3], [5].

## VI. Expressive Power and the Meaning of Aggregation

Xu, Hu, Leskovec, and Jegelka [4] examined a conceptual question that empirical accuracy alone cannot answer: which structural distinctions can a neighborhood-aggregation network represent? Their analysis connects the discriminative power of message-passing architectures to the Weisfeiler-Lehman graph isomorphism test. At a high level, iterative neighborhood refinement distinguishes nodes using the multiset of labels or representations in their surroundings. A graph network whose aggregation collapses distinct multisets may fail to recognize structural differences that could matter for the target task.

A mean aggregator loses multiplicity information in simple cases. A neighborhood containing one occurrence of a particular feature and a neighborhood containing several identical occurrences can have the same mean. A max aggregator can similarly ignore counts once its maximum is fixed. Sum aggregation has more potential to preserve multiset information when paired with a suitable learnable mapping. These examples show why the choice of invariant aggregator affects expressivity; they do not show that every empirically successful node-classification task requires a maximally discriminative aggregation operation.

The Graph Isomorphism Network, or GIN, uses a sum-based neighborhood combination followed by a multilayer perceptron, often expressed as h_v^(l+1)=MLP_l((1+epsilon_l)h_v^l + sum_{u in N(v)}h_u^l). The self-contribution parameter and expressive transformation support representation of distinctions that certain average-based mechanisms lose. The paper develops theoretical conditions for its expressive capability within the message-passing family and tests the model on graph-classification benchmarks [4]. The theory should be read with its assumptions: injective multiset representations depend on the function family and feature domain, and the analysis does not establish unconstrained expressivity for every possible graph computation.

GIN is especially informative in a survey centered on node classification because it separates two objectives often conflated in architecture comparisons. Graph classification asks for a representation of an entire graph and may require distinguishing global patterns across many local updates. Semi-supervised node classification predicts labels of particular vertices, sometimes in homophilous graphs where smoothing is more valuable than preserving every combinatorial distinction. A high-capacity multiset function can identify more structures in principle while still being harder to train or less robust when supervision is sparse. Consequently, expressivity should be treated as a design resource whose value depends on the prediction target.

This paper also changes how model comparisons should be argued. It is inadequate to claim that two layers are equivalent merely because they see two-hop neighborhoods. Two architectures can have the same receptive-field radius but differ in the information their aggregators retain. Conversely, a more expressive architecture can be restricted by inadequate node features, limited depth, or dataset construction. The decisive issue is not a ranking of model names; it is whether the chosen invariances and distinctions align with the task's signal [4].

## VII. Simplification as a Scientific Control

Wu and colleagues [5] investigated whether repeated learned nonlinear transformations were necessary for the performance attributed to GCNs. Their Simple Graph Convolution, or SGC, removes intermediate nonlinearities and collapses successive linear weight transformations. Feature propagation becomes a fixed graph filter applied before a linear classifier. In a convenient notation, Z=softmax(S^K X Theta), where S is the normalized propagation operator, K is a chosen number of propagation steps, and Theta is the trained linear map.

This separation has practical and interpretive consequences. When S^K X can be calculated once, repeated optimization steps no longer need to traverse the full graph for feature mixing. Training then resembles a conventional classifier on graph-filtered features. The model also makes a low-pass smoothing interpretation clearer: neighborhoods are used to create transformed attributes, but learned nonlinear feature manipulation does not occur between diffusion steps. For graphs and tasks in which smoothing supplies most of the gain, this reduced model can remain competitive while simplifying training [5].

SGC should not be understood as a proof that nonlinearity is dispensable for graph learning in general. The approach deliberately sacrifices the ability to apply a new nonlinear transformation after every propagation stage. In settings requiring nonlinear interactions among structural and attribute signals, or adaptation of propagation at intermediate layers, that restriction may be important. Moreover, precomputation is not free. It requires access to the relevant graph and features, and substantial graph changes can require propagation to be repeated. A favorable training-time result on a fixed graph does not necessarily describe end-to-end cost in a streaming production environment.

The paper is particularly valuable as a methodological challenge to the field. When a complex GNN outperforms a raw-feature multilayer perceptron, the improvement may reflect topology-aware smoothing rather than the entire deep architecture. A strong experimental design should therefore include a feature-only baseline, a simple propagation-plus-linear baseline, and an appropriately tuned nonlinear GNN whenever feasible. Such controls isolate the contribution of graph structure from the contribution of depth and learned transformations. Without them, apparent architectural advances can be partly explained by differences in preprocessing or comparison strength.

SGC also helps interpret the later personalized propagation literature. Both lines of work question how tightly classification and graph diffusion must be coupled. SGC fixes a number of propagation steps and then learns a linear decision rule. APPNP uses a neural prediction function and a personalized diffusion process to extend relational influence while retaining a connection to initial predictions [6]. The two constructions make different compromises, but both show that useful graph learning need not require the naive stacking of many standard convolution layers.

## VIII. Decoupling Prediction and Personalized Propagation

Gasteiger (formerly Klicpera), Bojchevski, and Günnemann [6] developed the idea of predicting from features first and then propagating those predictions using a personalized PageRank mechanism. Their PPNP construction and its scalable approximation, APPNP, were motivated by the difficulty of obtaining long-range information with shallow message-passing layers and by the degradation that can occur when standard GCNs are made deeper. The architecture decouples a neural feature-to-prediction function from the diffusion operator used to spread predictive information through the graph.

An illustrative APPNP recurrence is H^(k+1)=(1-alpha) S H^k + alpha H^0, where H^0 represents initial neural predictions, S is normalized graph propagation, and alpha is a teleport or restart probability. After a chosen number of iterations, the model uses the propagated scores for classification. The restart term continually restores a portion of the original predictions, preventing the entire update from becoming unconstrained repeated averaging. This is conceptually different from inserting additional independent feature transformations at every propagation step.

The personalized PageRank perspective provides a principled control on effective receptive field. A node can draw information through paths of different lengths, while the restart coefficient moderates how strongly distant information is allowed to displace the node's original signal. A small alpha generally permits more extensive diffusion, while a larger alpha attaches more weight to the initial predictions. The number of iterations determines how accurately the approximation approaches the intended propagation behavior. These are substantive modeling parameters, and they should be selected on validation evidence rather than fixed solely because a prior paper used particular values [6].

The method reveals an important distinction between depth and range. A deep network may obtain a large neighborhood through repeated learned feature transformations, but long-range interaction can also be supplied by a dedicated propagation process placed after a shallow neural predictor. The latter can make optimization less sensitive to many stacks of learned transformations. However, long-range access is not automatically beneficial. In a graph whose distant neighborhoods connect unrelated classes, additional diffusion can contaminate predictions, and a restart mechanism cannot fully compensate for a fundamentally unsuitable edge pattern.

APPNP and SGC can be analyzed together as two strategies for separating representation learning from propagation. SGC propagates input features before fitting a relatively simple predictor, while APPNP predicts from attributes before propagating scores. The difference affects what information is diffused, what parameters are optimized, and how graph changes are handled. Rather than calling one method deeper or more advanced, the appropriate comparison asks which signal should be smoothed: raw or transformed attributes, intermediate embeddings, or class evidence. This choice depends on feature semantics, label structure, and availability of relational information at inference time.

## IX. Over-Smoothing and the Limits of Depth

Oono and Suzuki [7] supplied a theoretical analysis of why stacking graph convolution-like layers can reduce a network's ability to distinguish nodes. Under conditions involving the normalized graph operator and the layer weights, propagated features approach a restricted space associated with structural properties such as graph components and node degrees. Their result is not a universal theorem that every deep graph network must fail, but it formalizes a mechanism by which successive propagation can remove discriminative variation. The analysis gives mathematical substance to the empirical observation that deeper message passing is often difficult to use effectively.

To understand the mechanism, consider repeated application of a smoothing operator to an initial graph signal. Components aligned with eigenvectors that are attenuated by the operator decay relative to components that persist. If the remaining signal is insufficient to separate nodes with different labels, a classifier receives progressively less useful information. Learned weights and nonlinearities complicate this picture, but they do not make the interaction between graph mixing and feature geometry disappear. The theoretical contribution of [7] is to identify conditions for a collapse in discriminative capacity, not to provide a single practical depth threshold valid for all architectures.

Over-smoothing should also be distinguished from over-squashing. The former concerns representations becoming insufficiently distinguishable after repeated mixing. The latter, discussed in the wider field, concerns excessive information being compressed through limited-dimensional representations or graph bottlenecks. Both can appear when expanding receptive fields, but they imply different interventions. Increasing hidden width, modifying topology, changing the propagation operator, residual connections, normalization, or decoupled diffusion may have different effects. A survey that treats every deep-GNN failure as the same phenomenon risks prescribing the wrong remedy.

The Oono-Suzuki perspective supports a pragmatic baseline strategy. Begin with a shallow model whose behavior can be inspected, then increase propagation radius only when a task-specific hypothesis requires distant information. Monitor performance by node degree and graph region, not just average accuracy. Assess whether embeddings lose variance or class separation as depth grows. If deeper layers fail, determine whether the problem is optimization, redundant smoothing, limited training labels, or incompatible neighborhood structure before introducing another architectural component.

In combination with APPNP [6] and SGC [5], over-smoothing theory reframes a common misconception: using more graph edges through larger receptive fields does not necessarily mean learning more useful information. Useful range depends on how propagation is normalized, how original information is preserved, and whether the class signal remains aligned with the features that survive aggregation. Depth is therefore a hypothesis to test, not a capacity dial that can be turned upward without consequence.

## X. Scalability Through Graph Sampling

GraphSAINT, developed by Zeng and colleagues [8], addresses the practical difficulty of training GNNs on graphs too large for straightforward full-batch computation. Conventional minibatch neighborhood methods select output nodes and recursively gather the neighbors required by each layer. As depth and degree increase, the set of required ancestors can grow substantially. GraphSAINT changes the unit of sampling: it draws a subgraph and runs the complete GNN within that sampled structure. Training operates on graph fragments whose size can be controlled more directly.

The paper considers several sampling schemes, including sampling nodes, edges, and random-walk-related structures [8]. These procedures differ in which connections and local contexts they preserve. Sampling individual nodes may omit many edges among relevant vertices, while path-oriented methods may retain connected local regions. A sampled subgraph is not a neutral slice of the original graph; the probability that a node or edge appears can affect the magnitude and variance of estimated learning signals. GraphSAINT consequently incorporates normalization ideas to correct sampling-induced bias and improve estimation.

A systems-level benefit is that sampled-subgraph computation can reuse the same local topology across all layers within a minibatch rather than repeatedly constructing an expanding frontier for every output node. This may improve data locality and permit deeper models under a fixed memory budget. However, there remains a tradeoff between subgraph size, structural coverage, training variance, and total iterations required. GraphSAINT is a training framework capable of supporting multiple message-passing architectures; it should not be interpreted as a new aggregation function directly comparable to GCN or GAT in isolation.

The distinction matters when reporting evidence. If GraphSAINT with one architecture beats a layer-sampled system with another, the observed difference cannot be assigned entirely to the sampler. To isolate effects, comparisons should hold the model, target metric, split, and optimization budget as constant as possible. Runtime claims also require clarity about data-loading cost, preprocessing, device transfers, and graph sampler implementation. A method can reduce accelerator memory consumption while moving work to a CPU sampler, which changes the best choice for a given deployment environment.

GraphSAGE and GraphSAINT represent two complementary approaches to reducing graph computation. GraphSAGE typically bounds per-layer neighborhood sizes around selected root nodes [2]. GraphSAINT bounds work by constructing sampled induced or related subgraphs [8]. The relative efficiency of each depends on graph degree distributions, the number of layers, caching, sampler throughput, and the density of training targets. Selecting a scalable algorithm means measuring the full data pipeline instead of extrapolating only from asymptotic formulas.

## XI. Reliable Evaluation and the Open Graph Benchmark

Hu and colleagues [9] introduced the Open Graph Benchmark (OGB) to confront limitations in commonly used graph-learning benchmarks. Small datasets with highly reused splits can make progress appear more settled than it is, especially when many research decisions have been adapted to those benchmarks over time. OGB supplies a diverse collection of graph tasks, more demanding scales, and standardized evaluation protocols, including application-relevant splits and metrics. Its contribution is methodological as well as infrastructural: it changes what researchers can reasonably claim from an accuracy comparison.

Graph tasks differ in what constitutes an independent future observation. A random node split may permit information from highly related nodes to appear in both training and evaluation. In a scientific citation network, a temporal split may more closely approximate prediction for future publications. In a biological setting, distribution shifts across species, protein families, or experimental conditions may be more consequential than a random holdout. OGB emphasizes that train/test partitioning must reflect the generalization question rather than being selected only for convenience [9].

The benchmark also broadens attention beyond tiny citation graphs. Datasets spanning larger information networks, molecules, biology, and other domains reveal bottlenecks that are invisible when a whole graph fits comfortably in memory. Some tasks evaluate node classification, while others evaluate edges or whole graphs. Their metrics are not interchangeable, and a model's behavior can differ sharply between them. It is therefore misleading to combine improvements from unrelated datasets into a single narrative of general architectural superiority without specifying the prediction unit, feature types, and split semantics.

A reproducible evaluation reports more than an accuracy value. At minimum it should specify graph version, preprocessing, edge direction and self-loop handling, split, seed, optimizer, training duration, model selection, hardware, and software versions. When possible, repeated runs should provide a mean and dispersion, especially if improvements are small. Comparisons should include simple and competitive baselines, since large architectures can benefit from tuning or preprocessing budgets unavailable to a weaker comparison method. OGB supports common infrastructure, but standardization cannot substitute for careful experimental conduct.

For the code in this project, the Cora Planetoid protocol is selected to teach the baseline, not to establish state-of-the-art performance. Its public masks offer a convenient entry point and its manageable size makes debugging straightforward. To pursue claims about scalable or realistic graph classification, the next stage should move to a suitable OGB dataset with its prescribed split and evaluator, record resource consumption, and repeat experiments across seeds. This progression respects the distinction between a pedagogical replication and an empirical research contribution [9].

## XII. Beyond Homophily: Structural and Geometric Context

Pei and colleagues' Geom-GCN [10] addresses the problem that many graph convolutional methods rely implicitly on neighboring nodes carrying similar information. Certain networks link unlike rather than like objects. In a web graph, a page may link to a complementary category; in a biological network, interacting proteins need not share a functional label; in a transactional network, connected roles can be systematically different. If direct averaging erases those differences, a model can perform poorly even though topology is informative. The relevant information may lie in the pattern of unlike connections rather than agreement among adjacent labels.

Geom-GCN explores geometric relationships and structured aggregation to capture contexts that ordinary neighborhood smoothing can miss [10]. Its design uses node embeddings in a latent space and organizes graph neighborhoods to provide multiple structural views. The core lesson is not that an additional geometric representation universally fixes heterophily. It is that the definition of a useful neighborhood is itself a modeling problem. Two nodes can be informative for one another because of structural roles or position even when their class labels differ.

This insight also changes how one evaluates an apparent failure of GCN. Poor accuracy might arise from weak node features, limited labeled data, a split that induces shift, or a graph whose edges join different classes. These mechanisms should be assessed separately. Homophily measurements should also be interpreted carefully: a global scalar can hide substantial variation by class, node degree, or graph region. Some vertices may benefit from local averaging while others require directional, relation-specific, or distant signals. A model that adapts across those regimes could be preferable to one tuned to the graph-wide majority.

Geom-GCN therefore provides a necessary counterweight to a simple reading of GCN and APPNP. Longer or stronger smoothing is beneficial only when the propagated signal is useful. Attention may reweight neighbors, but if the required distinction involves structural roles not inferable from local features alone, more expressive neighborhood construction may be needed. Conversely, an algorithm developed for heterophily should still be compared to straightforward attribute-only and graph-filtering baselines, since not every graph demands the same structural machinery.

The trajectory from GCN through Geom-GCN does not establish a single optimal method. It exposes a hierarchy of assumptions: whether edges are relevant, whether neighbors are alike, whether their contributions should be uniform, whether important patterns require multiset counts or structural positions, and whether graph information is stable between training and deployment. Each assumption can be tested empirically. This is more informative than selecting a network by popularity or the largest number in a published benchmark table.

## XIII. Comparative Synthesis: What Changes from One Method to Another?

A useful comparison begins by separating architectural axes rather than arranging papers into a leaderboard. GCN specifies a normalized propagation operator with learned transforms; GraphSAGE makes neighborhood aggregation a transferable function and introduces bounded neighborhood sampling; GAT learns feature-dependent edge coefficients; GIN emphasizes injective multiset aggregation; SGC removes intermediate nonlinearities; and APPNP separates feature-based predictions from iterative diffusion [1]–[6]. These models alter different mechanisms. A statement that one is more complex or more powerful cannot replace a precise account of the mechanism changed and the outcome it is supposed to improve.

The most basic axis is what gets aggregated. GCN mixes transformed features through normalized edge weights. GraphSAGE aggregates sampled neighbors with mean, pooling, or other functions before producing new features. GAT adds learned compatibility scores to the mixing stage. GIN sums elements of a neighborhood multiset and applies an expressive map. SGC performs fixed graph filtering over input features prior to classification, whereas APPNP diffuses a model's predictions. The choice determines whether information can be filtered nonlinearly at every layer, whether neighbor count is preserved, and how readily a classifier can be adapted to changing graph structure. It also influences what an engineer should inspect when a method fails: attention distributions, representation variance, propagation spectra, or sampling coverage.

A second axis concerns where supervision enters the system. In a conventional semi-supervised GCN, labels at training nodes determine the shared feature transformation through backpropagation. Neighbor features from unlabeled nodes may influence a labeled node's output and therefore the gradient, even though the unlabeled node's true class is never used in the loss. This is legitimate under the stated transductive protocol, but it would not correspond to a system that forbids access to future-node attributes. GraphSAGE's inductive design clarifies how training and deployment can be aligned when nodes arrive later. APPNP shows that label-trained predictions can be the objects propagated after a separate neural mapping. None of these decisions is intrinsically fairer or stronger: validity depends on the information that would exist at prediction time.

A third axis is the statistical relationship between edge formation and the label. Many standard citation benchmarks reward low-pass smoothing because related papers often cite one another. Under this pattern, GCN and even SGC can exploit local class coherence effectively. When edges carry complementary or role-based information, smoothing may be a poor prior. Geom-GCN and the broader heterophily literature motivate alternatives that condition on structural contexts instead of simply encouraging neighbors to look alike [10]. GAT can choose among immediate neighbors but cannot invent missing structural evidence, and an overly smooth APPNP predictor can still diffuse errors. The graph's label-mixing pattern is thus a first-order diagnostic, not a minor dataset characteristic.

A fourth axis is information preservation. Mean normalization removes some degree-driven scale and improves numerical behavior, but it can collapse distinct neighborhood multisets. GIN's expressivity analysis explains why preserving counts can matter [4]. Yet indiscriminate preservation of every distinction may also fit irrelevant training peculiarities. This creates a bias-variance question: should an architecture constrain nearby nodes toward similar embeddings, or retain structural differences that might only be weakly supervised? On a sparse-label dataset, stronger inductive bias can outperform a theoretically more expressive family. The architectural decision should be supported by ablations and data diagnostics rather than by a theoretical ranking alone.

Propagation distance forms a fifth axis. Adding GCN layers increases the maximum number of hops through which features can affect a node, but repeated mixing can diminish separability [7]. APPNP provides a different approach: an independently trained predictor is combined with multiple diffusion steps anchored by restart [6]. SGC precomputes a fixed number of smoothing steps before classification [5]. These models suggest experiments that vary receptive-field radius independently of the number of learned transformations. Such an experiment can reveal whether gains come from reach, nonlinear capacity, or the interaction between the two. Without this separation, a claim that additional depth helps may be causally ambiguous.

Scalability and performance metrics form a sixth axis. GraphSAGE's neighbor fanouts bound work around sampled targets; GraphSAINT samples subgraphs and corrects for sampling behavior [2], [8]. Neither is merely a low-level implementation of another model. Sampling changes the data distribution presented to the learner, making convergence and model quality dependent on graph exploration. SGC may offer inexpensive repeated optimization after precomputation, but that advantage depends on the graph being relatively stable. GAT incurs per-edge compatibility computation, which may be worthwhile if adaptive weighting improves accuracy enough to justify its cost. The correct operational objective might be test accuracy under a memory budget, latency at an acceptable error, training throughput, or stability when edges update; the papers do not optimize all of these objectives simultaneously.

Finally, benchmarking provides the axis on which every other conclusion rests. OGB [9] reminds the field that measured differences depend on problem size, realistic partitioning, and standardized evaluators. A two-layer GCN on Planetoid Cora and a sampled GNN on an industrial-scale evolving graph address different workloads. Their raw accuracy values cannot be compared directly, and even identical metric names may conceal different class distributions or split policies. Cross-paper synthesis is strongest when it compares mechanisms and controlled observations, and weakest when it presents numbers from different experimental regimes as evidence of a universal hierarchy.

## XIV. Critical Review of Research Design and Evidence

The surveyed papers make different kinds of scientific claims. GCN and GAT are primarily architectural proposals supported by experiments [1], [3]. GraphSAGE combines a model family with an inductive learning setting and sampling strategy [2]. GIN and the Oono-Suzuki study emphasize representational and mathematical analysis [4], [7]. SGC is a simplification and control argument [5]. APPNP introduces a particular diffusion construction [6]. GraphSAINT centers the efficiency and statistical properties of sampling [8]. OGB centers benchmark validity and infrastructure [9]. Geom-GCN challenges assumptions about graph topology and label relationships [10]. Evaluating them by a single notion of novelty would miss the different forms of evidence they contribute.

An architecture paper commonly demonstrates gains against chosen competitors, often on a finite set of public datasets. That evidence supports superiority within the specified experimental protocol, not universal superiority. Attention may be helpful when immediate neighbors have unequal predictive value, but a gain on several citation graphs does not show that the same scoring function is robust to noisy edges in arbitrary transactional or biological data. Likewise, a sampling system that reduces training time in one environment may have different bottlenecks on a graph stored remotely or a machine with relatively slow host-to-device transfers. The generality of an empirical finding should be inferred from the variation actually tested.

Theory papers require a complementary caution. The GIN analysis identifies distinctions that certain aggregators cannot represent, but finite neural networks trained with finite labels may not realize every theoretically available distinction [4]. Over-smoothing results depend on assumptions about operators, weights, and limits [7]. Their value is to reveal mechanisms and conditions; they do not replace predictive validation. A model can avoid a particular theoretical limitation yet perform poorly because its inductive bias is mismatched, optimization is unstable, or data quality is insufficient. Conversely, a theoretically limited model may be entirely adequate if the target categories do not depend on the unavailable distinctions.

Benchmark design can confound architectural comparisons through leakage, selection, and inappropriate controls. Label leakage occurs when information tied directly to withheld labels enters model input or training supervision through improper preprocessing. A transductive setting that permits unlabeled test features is not leakage merely because a training node is connected to a test node; it is part of the declared protocol. Problems arise when the intended deployment scenario would not make those features available, or when a preprocessing step indirectly reveals evaluation targets. Explicitly describing the allowed information makes that boundary auditable.

Hyperparameter search is another source of hidden asymmetry. A complex model may be granted dozens of tuning trials, while an older baseline is run once with untuned defaults. The resulting comparison conflates architecture with optimization effort. A fair evaluation should document search spaces, validation criteria, early-stopping policy, and computational budget. Where an exhaustive search is impossible, the limitation should be acknowledged rather than omitted. In classroom reproductions, a smaller but transparent search is usually more defensible than an undocumented search that produces a favorable value.

Statistical reporting deserves equal attention. An individual run is a realization of initialization, training dynamics, data splits, and sometimes neighbor sampling. Reporting the best run across several seeds inflates the perceived performance relative to a mean or a prespecified single seed. A credible multi-run comparison can report average test accuracy, standard deviation, and the number of trials, ideally after fixing model selection using only validation measurements. Confidence intervals may be useful, but must respect the dependencies among repeated runs on the same dataset. Reported decimals should not imply precision beyond the variation the experiment actually measures.

A separate problem is conflating parameter count with computational efficiency. Graph operations are sensitive to sparsity, memory layout, neighborhood sizes, and communication patterns. Two models with similar trainable parameters can have very different edge-processing requirements. To claim efficiency, measure wall-clock training duration under comparable conditions, peak accelerator memory, setup or preprocessing cost, and possibly energy or inference latency. If a method relies on substantial offline computation, that cost should not disappear from the analysis simply because it precedes the timed training loop. SGC and sampling-based systems make this accounting particularly important [5], [8].

Interpretability claims should be limited as well. A graph attention coefficient displays a learned relative weighting within a specified neighborhood, but it does not alone establish the causal contribution of an edge to a prediction. Removing an edge may alter normalization, surrounding embeddings, and all downstream computations. Similarly, a degree-normalized GCN coefficient is a design weight rather than an explanation of the target class. For explanations that guide decisions, one should test faithfulness by perturbation, analyze sensitivity to alternative graph constructions, and compare interpretations across seeds. This is especially important for high-stakes applications where a plausible visualization can mask unstable reasoning.

Taken together, these caveats produce a methodological rule: comparisons should isolate the mechanism under investigation and preserve the information-access and evaluation contracts. An experiment on attention should keep data, preprocessing, split, and optimization close to those of a fixed-weight aggregator. An experiment on sampling should ideally hold the message-passing architecture constant. A study of receptive-field size should avoid simultaneously increasing unrelated model capacity. Fully controlled comparisons may not always be possible, but identifying the confounds is a necessary part of scientific interpretation.

## XV. Baseline Reproduction: Design, Setup, and Measurements

The accompanying code implements the two-layer GCN because it is the simplest architecture in this survey that demonstrates the full node-classification training cycle while using graph structure directly [1]. PyTorch Geometric provides the `GCNConv` operator and the Planetoid dataset loaders. The official PyTorch Geometric repository contains a maintained public GCN example at https://github.com/pyg-team/pytorch_geometric/blob/master/examples/gcn.py . The project archive includes separately written training code so that the team can edit it and upload it to its own GitHub repository. The public URL documents an existing upstream example; it must not be presented as the team's repository.

The local implementation loads Cora by default and can also select CiteSeer or PubMed. It normalizes node features using the built-in transform, obtains the graph and public training/validation/test masks, and moves the graph tensors to a selected CPU or CUDA device. The first convolution maps the input feature dimension to a configurable hidden dimension. ReLU supplies nonlinearity, and dropout is applied around the graph convolutions during training. The second convolution returns one logit per class. Cross-entropy is calculated only on the nodes whose training-mask entries are true.

Training uses Adam with configurable learning rate and weight decay. At the end of each epoch, the model is switched to evaluation mode, and the validation mask is scored without dropout. The best validation accuracy determines which parameter snapshot will be retained. A patience counter stops training after a configurable number of epochs without validation improvement. When optimization finishes, the selected checkpoint is restored and the test accuracy is computed once for reporting. The script writes a history file with epoch-level training loss and training/validation accuracy, a JSON record of final evaluation and configuration fields, and a serialized parameter checkpoint. It does not fabricate or prepopulate metrics.

The recommended environment is Python 3.10 or newer with PyTorch, PyTorch Geometric, and NumPy. In an empty project directory, create a virtual environment with `python -m venv .venv`, activate it, install packages using `python -m pip install -r requirements.txt`, and run `python train.py --dataset Cora --epochs 200 --seed 42`. A first execution requires network access to download the public Planetoid dataset unless files are already cached. The `--device cpu` option forces CPU execution; CUDA can be selected on a compatible installation. Package and GPU wheel compatibility should be checked against the current official installation guides rather than inferred from a single frozen environment.

Output should be interpreted at the scale and protocol of the experiment. The training and validation curves can show whether the model overfits, whether optimization stagnates, or whether early stopping is aggressive. A single test accuracy is a descriptive result for one run, not evidence that the implementation exactly reproduces every number in [1]. To characterize variability, rerun the code with several independently specified seeds and compute a summary after all runs have completed. Do not choose a preferred seed using test accuracy. For a scientific report, include command lines, hardware, library versions, and a table of actual measured results alongside the split definition.

The code uses a full-graph transductive computation. The training mask ensures that only observed training labels contribute directly to supervised loss, but all nodes can participate in propagation through edges and features. This distinction must appear in any discussion of generalization. Running the same model on held-out nodes in Cora does not demonstrate GraphSAGE-style induction to an unseen graph. If the project later evaluates inductive generalization, it should construct separate graph observations or a temporal protocol, control what information is present at inference, and implement the relevant sampled-aggregation approach rather than relabeling a transductive test as inductive.

A controlled extension of the baseline would test a feature-only multilayer perceptron, SGC-like precomputed smoothing, and APPNP-like output diffusion under the same split. The feature-only model identifies the value of graph access. The simplified propagation model asks whether intermediate nonlinear graph operations are necessary [5]. APPNP tests whether longer-range propagation anchored to initial predictions adds value [6]. Attention, GraphSAGE sampling, and alternative neighbor construction can then be added one factor at a time. Such an ablation sequence is more informative than implementing many models with inconsistent preprocessing and reporting only whichever one performs best.

Experimental recordkeeping should be treated as part of the baseline rather than an optional afterthought. Save the configuration, software versions, seed, final checkpoint, and raw per-epoch measurements. Explain if the selected metric is accuracy or a different measure suitable for class imbalance. For multiple datasets, avoid pooling all nodes into a single aggregate that obscures graph-specific behavior. When new experiments are added, record failed or inconclusive trials as well as successful ones, so model selection and resource expenditure remain visible. These practices give the baseline value as a reproducible research artifact even when it does not introduce a novel algorithm.

## XVI. Applications, Deployment Conditions, and Failure Modes

The surveyed architectures apply to many graph-rich settings, but their assumptions change meaning across domains. In a citation network, features may be bag-of-words or learned document embeddings, edges represent citations, and the class may denote a research area. Here document content and neighborhood context can reinforce one another. In a recommendation setting, edges may connect distinct entity types, such as users and products, and label similarity across neighbors is not an appropriate global assumption. A direct transplant of homogeneous GCN smoothing can then mix quantities that do not share semantics, unless the graph and feature representation are carefully constructed.

In a security or fraud-detection context, graph edges may connect accounts, devices, transactions, and institutions. Connections can be deliberately manipulated, and model outputs may influence adversarial behavior. Simply training a deeper network to include more hops can expose the model to additional contaminated evidence. A defensible deployment would examine edge provenance, changing behavior over time, severe class imbalance, and the cost of false positives. Node labels may become available only after lengthy review, making leakage across timestamps a significant concern. The principal issue is the alignment of data collection with prediction time, not whether the selected model name is GCN or GAT.

Biological interaction graphs present a different challenge. A protein's functional label may not agree with the labels of its interacting partners, and experimentally observed edges may have confidence levels or assay-specific biases. Graph attention may learn to differentiate some neighbors, while role-aware or heterophily-sensitive approaches may be more suitable for particular tasks. Yet confidence scores and interaction types should be incorporated carefully rather than treated as if all edges were identical. A published gain on a citation network does not establish biological validity. Domain-specific benchmarks, known confounders, and external validation are required.

Production graphs are rarely static. New vertices arrive, existing edges disappear, node features change, and labels are delayed. Full-graph feature propagation may be inexpensive on a small snapshot but too costly to repeat on every update. Cached SGC features can become stale [5]. Sampling methods may permit minibatch training, but inference still needs a strategy for collecting neighborhoods at serving time [2], [8]. Personalized diffusion can use a wider region but requires updating or approximating propagation after graph changes [6]. The appropriate system design must include update cadence, cache invalidation, latency, consistency, and behavior during partial graph availability.

Evaluation should also monitor distributions rather than only graph-wide means. Low-degree, isolated, or newly created nodes have less relational evidence and may rely heavily on attributes. High-degree nodes experience large and potentially mixed neighborhoods. Minoritized classes may be surrounded by majority-class nodes, creating a risk that homophily-based smoothing suppresses their signals. Domain and group-specific evaluation can reveal harms concealed by an overall accuracy score. Such subgroup analysis does not itself establish fairness, but it is a necessary step toward understanding whose predictions improve or deteriorate under relational learning.

Robustness to edge errors is especially important. An erroneous connection changes the messages that a node receives, while missing edges reduce access to useful evidence. The consequences can propagate over multiple layers. Some architectures learn neighbor weights, but learned weights can be unstable or rely on the very features that an attacker or data error has distorted. Stress tests should add, remove, or perturb plausible edges; simulate feature missingness; and examine performance changes across graph regions. Robustness should be measured under realistic perturbation budgets, not asserted from the mere presence of an attention module.

Privacy presents a separate constraint. Relational information can disclose connections and properties of people who never supplied labeled examples. Even if the supervised loss uses only training labels, propagation may incorporate attributes from other vertices. Depending on the application, the training pipeline may need access controls, aggregation limits, data minimization, auditing, or privacy-preserving learning methods. A model that is statistically valid under a public research dataset is not automatically lawful or appropriate for a sensitive production graph. Data governance should be settled before graph construction and feature propagation, not appended after optimization.

These deployment issues connect back to the central scientific distinction between model and protocol. A correct implementation of an algorithm does not imply a correct product. GraphSAGE matters when new nodes need timely embeddings. GAT matters when variable neighbor reliability is supported by available features. SGC matters when repeated training on a stable graph is costly. APPNP matters when long-range relationships are useful but deep learned transformations are difficult. GraphSAINT matters when large graph storage and minibatching dominate resource use. Geom-GCN matters when ordinary edge neighborhoods are structurally informative without being label-homogeneous. The correct choice depends on measured needs rather than the chronology of publications.

## XVII. Open Problems and Research Directions

A recurring open problem is matching the graph operator to local, rather than average, relational patterns. Real networks may contain homophilous clusters, heterophilous bridges, hubs, and structurally equivalent vertices with no direct connections. A single globally applied low-pass filter may be poorly calibrated across these regimes. Future designs can investigate adaptive mixtures of propagation operators, but flexibility should be justified by improvements on targeted diagnostic subsets. Otherwise a more elaborate operator risks obscuring the same fundamental uncertainty about which edges carry task-relevant information.

Another research direction is the separation of topology quality from architecture quality. Graph datasets are often accepted as fixed inputs even though their edge-generation process can be incomplete, noisy, or biased. Learned edge selection and graph rewiring may improve predictions, but they also expand the hypothesis space and create opportunities for leakage. A strong study should evaluate sensitivity to plausible alternative graph constructions, compare against feature-only models, and ensure that any learned graph transformation uses only information available at the prediction time. Improved results after rewiring are meaningful only if the procedure itself is reproducible and respects the split.

The relation between expressivity, generalization, and sample efficiency also remains central. GIN-style multiset discrimination [4] offers a principled way to preserve structural differences, while GCN-like smoothing may supply valuable bias under sparse labels [1]. A model that can distinguish many possible neighborhoods may need stronger supervision to identify which distinctions predict the target. Rigorous work should therefore study performance as the number of observed labels changes, report sensitivity to feature noise, and separate capacity from effective sample complexity. Such analyses could explain why simple architectures remain competitive despite more sophisticated representational alternatives.

Scalable experimentation will require better integration of sampling statistics and hardware measurements. GraphSAINT demonstrates that the sampling unit can shape training efficiency [8], and GraphSAGE shows the deployment relevance of inductive sampling [2]. Yet sampler choice can affect variance, the frequency with which rare structures are encountered, and the accuracy of gradient estimates. Future comparisons should vary graph size and degree skew, document sampling distributions, and include data movement as part of measured runtime. A benchmark that only reports accelerator computation time can incorrectly reward methods whose expensive preprocessing is hidden elsewhere.

Temporal graph learning is another necessary extension beyond fixed citation snapshots. Edges and features can evolve between model training and evaluation, and the graph can contain evidence that did not yet exist at the time an earlier prediction would have been made. Temporal splits and timestamp-respecting neighborhood construction are necessary for meaningful deployment simulations. The formal distinction between transductive and inductive settings becomes more subtle when nodes persist but their relationships change. Evaluation protocols must specify not only which vertices are new, but what was knowable at each moment.

Uncertainty quantification is insufficiently captured by top-one node-classification accuracy. Graph propagation can amplify a locally incorrect prediction across correlated neighbors, creating clusters of confident errors. Calibration tests, selective prediction, and robustness analyses would help determine whether output probabilities are trustworthy. These measurements are especially important when decisions are costly or explanations are required. A low average error rate does not guarantee that the model identifies high-risk cases or recognizes neighborhoods unlike those seen during training.

Benchmark evolution remains indispensable. OGB demonstrates how standardized splits and evaluation tools can raise methodological standards [9], but every benchmark can become overused. Continued progress depends on datasets with realistic heterophily, temporal variation, domain shifts, scale, and documented collection processes. Researchers should resist optimizing exclusively for familiar public datasets. More revealing claims may come from failure analysis, stronger baselines, and careful evaluation under distribution shift than from a marginal improvement on a saturated split.

Finally, reproducibility must encompass both software and argument. A public repository makes code available but does not guarantee that the reported result can be reproduced without the correct dataset, environment, and model-selection procedure. Likewise, a long literature review is not a scientific synthesis unless it distinguishes assumptions, evidence, and limitations across papers. The strongest continuation of this project would pair a transparent baseline with a small, predeclared set of comparative hypotheses, then report all measured outcomes and computational costs. Such a study could contribute useful empirical evidence without claiming the invention of a new GNN architecture.

## XVIII. Conclusion

Graph neural networks provide a flexible framework for learning from structured relations, but the surveyed literature shows that their apparent simplicity conceals several distinct questions. GCN demonstrates how normalized local propagation can support semi-supervised node classification [1]. GraphSAGE extends neighborhood aggregation toward inductive, sampled settings [2]. GAT makes neighbor contributions data-dependent [3], and GIN clarifies the structural distinctions different aggregators can represent [4]. SGC and APPNP challenge the necessity of tightly coupled deep nonlinear propagation [5], [6]. Over-smoothing theory explains conditions under which increasing depth reduces node discrimination [7], while GraphSAINT addresses the computational organization of large-scale training [8]. OGB emphasizes reproducible and realistic evaluation [9], and Geom-GCN makes clear that useful edges do not always connect same-label nodes [10].

The central conclusion is conditional rather than prescriptive. The most suitable architecture is determined by the graph's structure, the nature and timing of observed features, the required generalization setting, and resource constraints. A shallow model can be superior when smoothing matches the label signal; adaptive attention or structural aggregation can help when neighborhood evidence varies; sampled learning can make otherwise impractical computation feasible; and better evaluation can overturn claims that appeared decisive on a narrow benchmark. For this reason, future graph-learning experiments should include strong simple controls, explicit information-access assumptions, reproducible splits, and transparent reporting of training and testing procedures.

The accompanying PyTorch baseline operationalizes this position. It provides a complete, editable two-layer GCN with setup instructions and structured output but does not report invented experimental results. The result is a foundation for independent measurement and a coherent reading of the field rather than a claim of new algorithmic performance.
